Chapter One

One Command, Five Phases

/critique_doc <document> [quick|deep]

You hand it a strategy document. It hands you back a verdict on every load-bearing claim — each one attacked by a specialist, defended by a devil's advocate for the defense, and stress-tested by an independent verifier before it reaches your desk.

The pipeline borrows a discipline from engineering review: the coordinator never writes the critique itself. A lead agent extracts the document, catalogs its claims, and then delegates every judgment to specialists who work in parallel. Anything severe enough to embarrass the author gets independently re-checked before it is allowed into the final report.

If you're new to this
Think of it as convening a review board on demand. Instead of waiting weeks to get a market analyst, a finance person, an architect, and a fact-checker into one room, the command spins up an AI specialist for each role, has them read the same document simultaneously, and requires every criticism to cite the exact quote it attacks and a source anyone can check.
Chapter Two

First, Turn Prose into Claims

Phase 1 · claims-register.md

You cannot review a vibe. Before anyone criticizes anything, the document is decomposed into discrete, quotable, load-bearing claims — each with an ID.

The register is the contract for the whole review. Each claim records the exact quote (never a paraphrase), where it lives in the document, what kind of claim it is, and which specialists will examine it. From the FinOps run:

IDTypeClaim (verbatim, elided)Assigned lenses
C-01market"There is no single product in the market that solves this problem…"market-analysis, logic-rhetoric
C-03factual"GPT-4 … costed $20/million tokens in 2022, today it costs $0.40/million … ~200x less"citation-audit, economics
C-08economic"compare projected cost … on-prem versus in public cloud … before committing budget"economics, feasibility
C-13economic"Idle or Underutilized VM Detection … shut down, resize, or consolidate"economics, market-analysis

Six claim types cover most strategy documents: factual (numbers, dates, prices, citations), market ("no product exists"), economic (cost logic, monetization), technical-feasibility (integration promises), customer-persona (who buys, who acts), and logical-inference ("X therefore Y"). Every factual claim automatically gets the fact-checker; every claim gets the defense advocate.

Merge micro-claims that stand or fall together; split compound claims whose parts have independent truth values. A 5–10 page strategy doc typically yields 8–16 claims. — The register discipline
Chapter Three

Seven Lenses, One Document

Phase 2 · parallel specialists

Each specialist reads the whole document but attacks only the claims tagged for its lens. Six prosecute. One — deliberately — defends.

citation-audit
every number, date, price, footnote
Recomputes every ratio from the doc's own figures; checks whether the cited source type can even contain the claimed data. Live-verifies prices — never trusts memory.
market-analysis
"no product exists" & demand claims
Names actual competitors and fetches their current capabilities. Distinguishes "no product does this exact bundle" from "the need is unserved."
economics
cost logic & unit economics
Hunts CAPEX/OPEX conflation and marginal-vs-average confusion. Breaks weak cost logic with a worked counter-example, not an abstract objection.
feasibility
integration & plumbing promises
For every "detects automatically" or "no credentials re-entered," enumerates what must actually exist. Flags data-platform projects disguised as UI features.
persona
who buys, who acts
Checks whether the people who own the optimization levers are even in the persona table. A tool whose advice nobody is chartered to execute has no theory of action.
logic-rhetoric
the argument itself — doc text only
Non sequiturs, category collapses, self-cancelling principles, load-bearing TBDs. Cites the document against itself; no web research allowed.
steelman
every claim — the defense
Writes the strongest honest defense of every claim and the narrowed form that would survive hostile review. Where no defense exists, says so — that admission is signal.

Every finding follows one enforced format: the exact quote, a verdict on a five-step scale, the analysis with explicit arithmetic, an evidence table where every row is independently checkable, a remediation (the narrowed claim that survives), and a request to the author — a genuine question whose answer could change the verdict.

VerdictMeaning
FATALStructurally wrong; everything built on it inherits the error
SEVEREWrong or unsupportable as written; survives only materially narrowed
MATERIALReal weakness that changes conclusions at the margin
MINORImprecision worth fixing; argument intact
HOLDSChecked; it stands — conceded plainly
Why a defense advocate
A red-team that only attacks is a strawman generator. The steelman covers all sixteen claims and its output becomes the "what survives" section of the final report — the honest kernel the author can rebuild on. On the FinOps run it found a defensible narrowed form for every claim, which is exactly what makes the report usable rather than merely bruising.
Chapter Four

Attack the Attackers

Phase 3 · adversarial verification

The most dangerous output of any review is a confident, plausible, wrong criticism. So every severe finding is handed to a fresh agent whose only job is to refute it.

Verifiers re-run the arithmetic, re-fetch every cited source themselves, and check the quote against the document — did the specialist attack what the author actually wrote, or a strawman? Each returns one of three outcomes: confirmed, downgraded, or killed.

On the FinOps run the verifiers downgraded six of the eight findings they checked — catching real strawmen. One specialist had accused the document's guiding principles of contradicting each other; the verifier showed the document's own "velocity takes precedence" clause resolved the tension. Another had claimed a placement comparison was "systematically wrong"; the verifier noticed the finding's own worked example contradicted that. Both criticisms would have looked entirely credible in a report. Neither survived a hostile re-check.

The verification round downgraded six of eight checked findings. That is signal for the document, not against the review — the author's reasoning largely held up under adversarial scrutiny. What did not hold up was factual precision. — From the run's executive synthesis

And what verification confirms, it confirms hard. The two findings that survived were re-proven from scratch: GPT-4 launched in March 2023, not 2022; its real launch price was $30/M tokens, not $20; and the document's own figures ($20 ÷ $0.40) imply a 50x drop while the same sentence asserts 200x. A claim that fails an audit twice, independently, is safe to put in front of leadership.

Chapter Five

From Findings to a Working Session

Phase 4–5 · synthesis & PDF delivery

The final deliverable is not a takedown. It is the agenda for the next conversation with the author — verdicts, patterns, what survives, and specific asks.

One synthesizer agent reads everything — the register, all seven lens reports, the verification notes — and writes two documents: a full executive synthesis and a one-page executive summary for leadership. The scoreboard leads:

IDClaimVerdictThe kill shot
C-01"No single product spans private + public FinOps"SEVERECloudHealth and Flexera already sell unified on-prem + cloud FinOps today
C-03GPT-4 "$20/M in 2022 → $0.40/M, ~200x less"SEVEREThe doc's own $20 ÷ $0.40 = 50x, not 200x; GPT-4 launched in 2023 at $30/M
C-13VM optimization savings (US-5)SEVERE"Shut down idle VM → save $280/mo" ignores prepaid commitments — net savings ≈ $0
C-08On-prem vs. cloud placement (US-3)MATERIALComparing sunk CAPEX against cloud on-demand needs a stated cost basis the doc never gives
C-15"Monetization path is TBD"MINORStage-appropriate for a vision doc — verified and downgraded from SEVERE

Below the scoreboard, the synthesis names the systemic patterns — the few defect classes that generate most of the individual findings. On the FinOps run, one cost-methodology decision (marginal cost for placement decisions, amortized for chargeback, net-of-commitment for optimization) resolved four findings at once. Fixing pattern classes beats patching sixteen claims one at a time.

Then the collaborative machinery — because the audience is the author, not a tribunal:

  • ASK
    Clarifications requested

    Six questions where the author's answer changes a verdict — e.g., "Is the $63k→$86k figure from CloudZero rather than the FinOps survey? If yes, this softens to MINOR — only the footnote needs re-attribution."

  • COMMENT
    Observations open for comment

    Findings where the panel may have missed context, stated as "we read §X as… — please confirm or correct," with an offer to re-verify.

  • MEET
    Suggested working session

    A four-item agenda ordered by leverage: cost methodology first (~45 min, resolves four findings), then release-scope lines, then a 20-minute citation cleanup that restores the document's credibility.

Everything renders to PDF into a dated folder — a leadership one-pager, the full synthesis, the claims register, seven deep-dives, and one combined file for circulation. Markdown working copies sit alongside for the next revision cycle.

Chapter Six

Why It Works This Way

Four design choices carry the whole thing.

  • 01
    The coordinator never critiques

    The lead extracts, registers, deploys, and reports — but writes no verdicts. Every judgment comes from a specialist with a narrow charter, so no single perspective (or single model's blind spot) dominates the review.

  • 02
    Exact quotes and checkable evidence, or it doesn't count

    "I believe this is wrong" is not a finding. Every verdict cites the verbatim text it attacks; every factual refutation cites a source anyone can open. Claims that can't be verified either way are capped at MATERIAL and labeled UNVERIFIED.

  • 03
    Severe criticism must survive its own red-team

    Verification is the review reviewing itself. On the FinOps run it filtered out six overstated findings that would each have looked convincing in a report — the difference between a credible review and a confident one.

  • 04
    The severity scale carries the judgment; the prose carries the partnership

    Verdicts are unflinching, but every deliverable ends in questions, comment requests, and a standing offer to re-verify against new data. The output is the opening of a dialogue with the author, not a verdict handed down.

What a PM actually gets
Roughly 25 minutes after handing over the document: a one-page summary saying what holds and what doesn't, a scoreboard with the evidence one click deep, the short list of questions that would change the verdicts, and a prioritized agenda for the revision working session. The full run against the FinOps doc deployed 16 agents and produced 11 PDFs — with zero claims declared beyond rescue.
