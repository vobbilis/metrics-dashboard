Aigile Playbook · Document Review

The Critique Pipeline
how a strategy doc gets red-teamed

One command deploys a panel of twelve registered AI specialist agents against a strategy document, hunts what it fails to say as hard as what it claims, adversarially verifies every severe finding, and returns a claim-by-claim scoreboard with a collaborative path back to the author. This is how it works — illustrated with a real run against a real FinOps strategy document.

Chapter One

One Command, Five Phases

/critique_doc <document> [quick|deep]

You hand it a strategy document. It hands you back a verdict on every load-bearing claim — each one attacked by a specialist, defended by a devil's advocate for the defense, and stress-tested by an independent verifier before it reaches your desk.

The pipeline borrows a discipline from engineering review: the coordinator never writes the critique itself. A lead agent extracts the document, catalogs its claims, and then delegates every judgment to specialists who work in parallel. Anything severe enough to embarrass the author gets independently re-checked before it is allowed into the final report.

If you're new to this
Think of it as convening a review board on demand. Instead of waiting weeks to get a market analyst, a finance person, an architect, and a fact-checker into one room, the command spins up an AI specialist for each role, has them read the same document simultaneously, and requires every criticism to cite the exact quote it attacks and a source anyone can check.
Chapter Two

First, Turn Prose into Claims

Phase 1 · claims-register.md

You cannot review a vibe. Before anyone criticizes anything, the document is decomposed into discrete, quotable, load-bearing claims — each with an ID.

The register is the contract for the whole review. Each claim records the exact quote (never a paraphrase), where it lives in the document, what kind of claim it is, and which specialists will examine it. From the FinOps run:

IDTypeClaim (verbatim, elided)Assigned lenses
C-01market"There is no single product in the market that solves this problem…"market-analysis, logic-rhetoric
C-04factual"GPT-4 … costed $20/million tokens in 2022, today it costs $0.40/million … ~200x less"citation-audit, economics
C-06feasibility"placement with model & infrastructure considered together for a given job"feasibility, economics
C-08logical"One entry point … for both workloads, infrastructure and public cloud use cases"feasibility, logic-rhetoric

Six claim types cover most strategy documents: factual (numbers, dates, prices, citations), market ("no product exists"), economic (cost logic, monetization), technical-feasibility (integration promises), customer-persona (who buys, who acts), and logical-inference ("X therefore Y"). Every factual claim automatically gets the fact-checker; every claim gets the defense advocate.

Merge micro-claims that stand or fall together; split compound claims whose parts have independent truth values. A 5–10 page strategy doc typically yields 8–16 claims. — The register discipline
Chapter Three

Eight Lenses, One Document

Phase 2 · parallel specialists

Each specialist reads the whole document but attacks only the claims tagged for its lens. Six prosecute the stated claims. One hunts the silences. One — deliberately — defends. A ninth, the product-craft reviewer, judges the craft rather than the truth.

citation-audit
every number, date, price, footnote
Recomputes every ratio from the doc's own figures; checks whether the cited source type can even contain the claimed data. Live-verifies prices — never trusts memory.
market-analysis
"no product exists" & demand claims
Names actual competitors and fetches their current capabilities. Distinguishes "no product does this exact bundle" from "the need is unserved."
economics
cost logic & unit economics
Hunts CAPEX/OPEX conflation and marginal-vs-average confusion. Breaks weak cost logic with a worked counter-example, not an abstract objection.
feasibility
integration & plumbing promises
For every "detects automatically" or "no credentials re-entered," enumerates what must actually exist. Flags data-platform projects disguised as UI features.
persona
who buys, who acts
Checks whether the people who own the optimization levers are even in the persona table. A tool whose advice nobody is chartered to execute has no theory of action.
logic-rhetoric
the argument itself — doc text only
Non sequiturs, category collapses, self-cancelling principles, load-bearing TBDs. Cites the document against itself; no web research allowed.
omissions
what the document doesn't say
The one lens not scoped to the register. Hunts six absence classes — adjacent categories already shipped, mechanism-free features, missing trust specs, thesis-vs-scope mismatch, decision-context gaps, load-bearing unmade decisions. Its findings get O-IDs and are verified like any claim.
steelman
every claim — the defense
Writes the strongest honest defense of every claim and the narrowed form that would survive hostile review. Ends with "the reconstructed strategy" — the strongest version built from the org's real assets. Where no defense exists, says so; that admission is signal.
product-craft
craft, not truth (Cagan / SVPG lens)
Judges the Four Big Risks (value, usability, feasibility, viability) and whether success is defined as an outcome or an output. Reviews the doc as a whole; its findings aren't falsifiable, so they skip verification and flow straight to synthesis.

Every finding follows one enforced format: the exact quote, a verdict on a five-step scale, the analysis with explicit arithmetic, an evidence table where every row is independently checkable, a remediation (the narrowed claim that survives), and a request to the author — a genuine question whose answer could change the verdict.

VerdictMeaning
FATALStructurally wrong; everything built on it inherits the error
SEVEREWrong or unsupportable as written; survives only materially narrowed
MATERIALReal weakness that changes conclusions at the margin
MINORImprecision worth fixing; argument intact
HOLDSChecked; it stands — conceded plainly
Why a defense advocate
A red-team that only attacks is a strawman generator. The steelman covers all sixteen claims and its output becomes the "what survives" section of the final report — the honest kernel the author can rebuild on. On the FinOps run it found a defensible narrowed form for every claim, which is exactly what makes the report usable rather than merely bruising.
Chapter Three · Appendix

The Registered Roster

~/.claude/agents/critique-*.md

The v2 refactor moved every specialist out of the orchestrator and into its own agent definition. Twelve files, each carrying its own charter, finding format, and reply shape — versioned, prefix-cached across runs, and individually invocable.

The coordinator's task prompts carry only run-specific paths; everything about how a lens works lives in its .md definition. That is what makes the panel composable — another command can borrow the fact-checker, and the contract between orchestrator and agents is a single versioned section rather than prose scattered through a 900-line prompt.

The contract that binds them
All twelve agents share one versioned interface — the finding format, the five-step severity scale, the C-IDs / O-IDs, and each agent's JSON reply shape. Change the contract and you bump its version and update every file that states it. It plays the same role a type surface plays in a build spec: the one artifact every worker agrees on.
Why nine attack but only six are "register-scoped"
Six lenses examine claims pulled from the register. Omissions can't — its whole subject is what the register could never contain, so it self-directs against the document. Steelman defends rather than prosecutes, and product-craft judges craft rather than truth. That division is deliberate: it was added after an early run matched a human review on every stated claim yet missed all four of the reviewer's best findings — because all four were absences.
Chapter Four

Attack the Attackers

Phase 3 · adversarial verification

The most dangerous output of any review is a confident, plausible, wrong criticism. So every severe finding is handed to a fresh agent whose only job is to refute it.

Verifiers re-run the arithmetic, re-fetch every cited source themselves, and check the quote against the document — did the specialist attack what the author actually wrote, or a strawman? Each returns one of three outcomes: confirmed, downgraded, or killed.

On the FinOps run the verifiers downgraded seven of the eight findings they checked — catching real strawmen. A feasibility finding had assumed a Morpheus credential carries no cost-data scope; the verifier found Morpheus costing actually ingests AWS Cost and Usage Report data on that same credential, and the SEVERE collapsed to MINOR. A persona finding had read an "answer-only" clause as hiding a chatbot's lineage; the verifier showed the clause merely scopes the feature read-only. Both criticisms would have looked entirely credible in a report. Neither survived a hostile re-check.

The verification round downgraded seven of eight checked findings. That is signal for the document, not against the review — the panel's first-pass severities ran hot, and only adversarial re-checking produced a defensible scoreboard. — From the run's executive synthesis

And what verification confirms, it confirms hard. The one finding that survived at full severity — O-06, an omission — was re-proven from scratch: nothing in the document defines a single identity key that maps a cost from Morpheus, HPE Flex, three public clouds, and token usage onto the same "business unit." Three of the six features that silently depend on it are P0. "By BU" is an output over an undefined dimension — unbuildable as specified. A finding that survives a hostile re-check by an agent paid to kill it is safe to put in front of leadership.

Chapter Five

From Findings to a Working Session

Phase 4–5 · synthesis & PDF delivery

The final deliverable is not a takedown. It is the agenda for the next conversation with the author — verdicts, patterns, what survives, and specific asks.

One synthesizer agent reads everything — the register, all nine lens reports, the verification notes — and writes two documents: a full executive synthesis and a one-page executive summary for leadership. The scoreboard leads:

IDClaim / omissionVerdictThe kill shot
O-06No cross-source BU/project identity model anywhereSEVEREFour sources, four native identity systems, nothing reconciles them — and 3 of 6 dependent features are P0. Survived verification intact.
C-04GPT-4 "$20/M in 2022 → $0.40/M, ~200x less"SEVEREThe doc's own $20 ÷ $0.40 = 50x, not 200x; GPT-4 launched in 2023 at $30/M — and the cited FinOps survey carries no token prices
C-01"No single product spans private + public FinOps"MATERIALFlexera One is a live counterexample, so "does not exist" fails — but the on-prem/Flex wedge survives. Downgraded from SEVERE.
O-01Names no incumbent; never separates moat from catch-upMATERIALVantage, CAST AI, and the FOCUS spec already ship most of US-4/5/7 — but the on-prem slice is genuinely unserved. Downgraded from SEVERE.
C-07Reuse Morpheus clouds, "no credentials re-entered"MINORPremise refuted — Morpheus costing ingests AWS CUR on the same credential. Downgraded from SEVERE.

Below the scoreboard, the synthesis names the systemic patterns — the few defect classes that generate most of the individual findings. On the FinOps run, one unmade architecture decision (the cross-source identity model, O-06) blocked four separate features at once, and one cost-methodology choice resolved several economics findings together. Fixing pattern classes beats patching claims one at a time.

Then the collaborative machinery — because the audience is the author, not a tribunal:

  • ASK
    Clarifications requested

    Six questions where the author's answer changes a verdict — e.g., "Is the $63k→$86k figure from CloudZero rather than the FinOps survey? If yes, this softens to MINOR — only the footnote needs re-attribution."

  • COMMENT
    Observations open for comment

    Findings where the panel may have missed context, stated as "we read §X as… — please confirm or correct," with an offer to re-verify.

  • MEET
    Suggested working session

    A four-item agenda ordered by leverage: cost methodology first (~45 min, resolves four findings), then release-scope lines, then a 20-minute citation cleanup that restores the document's credibility.

Everything renders to PDF into a dated folder — a leadership one-pager, the full synthesis, the commitment review, the claims register, nine deep-dives, and one combined file for circulation. Markdown working copies sit alongside for the next revision cycle.

Chapter Six

Why It Works This Way

Four design choices carry the whole thing.

  • 01
    The coordinator never critiques

    The lead extracts, registers, deploys, and reports — but writes no verdicts. Every judgment comes from a specialist with a narrow charter, so no single perspective (or single model's blind spot) dominates the review.

  • 02
    Exact quotes and checkable evidence, or it doesn't count

    "I believe this is wrong" is not a finding. Every verdict cites the verbatim text it attacks; every factual refutation cites a source anyone can open. Claims that can't be verified either way are capped at MATERIAL and labeled UNVERIFIED.

  • 03
    Severe criticism must survive its own red-team

    Verification is the review reviewing itself. On the FinOps run it filtered out six overstated findings that would each have looked convincing in a report — the difference between a credible review and a confident one.

  • 04
    The severity scale carries the judgment; the prose carries the partnership

    Verdicts are unflinching, but every deliverable ends in questions, comment requests, and a standing offer to re-verify against new data. The output is the opening of a dialogue with the author, not a verdict handed down.

What a PM actually gets
Roughly 25 minutes after handing over the document: a one-page summary saying what holds and what doesn't, a scoreboard with the evidence one click deep, the short list of questions that would change the verdicts, and a prioritized agenda for the revision working session, plus a per-story ship-readiness call so leadership funds commitments, not claims. The full run against the FinOps doc deployed a nine-agent panel, eight adversarial verifiers, a synthesizer, and a commitment reviewer, and produced 14 PDFs — with zero claims declared beyond rescue.
